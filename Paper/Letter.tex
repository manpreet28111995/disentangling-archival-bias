Dear Dr. Farina, Prof. Stahl, Dr. Webb, and Editorial Board,

We submit our original research manuscript, ``Archival Selection and Prompt-Relative Associations in Vision-Language Models for Museum Collections,'' for consideration in the special issue on ``Responsible use of AI in Art Creation and Archival Practice'' in \textit{AI \& Society}.

The paper examines whether apparent demographic differences in vision-language model scores may reflect the composition of museum metadata rather than a direct model association. We audit 1,500 records from the Metropolitan Museum of Art Open Access collection. After excluding records with unknown or unattributed creators and named-attribution records without usable images, the final evaluation cohort contains 743 works, including 534 male-inferred and 209 female-inferred records. Creator categories are inferred from metadata and names; they are name-inferred creator categories, not measures of personal gender identity.

We compare OpenAI CLIP, pretrained on OpenAI's WebImageText dataset, with OpenCLIP, pretrained on LAION-2B. Across prompt-relative score comparisons, neither model shows a statistically detectable association with the name-inferred creator category. TOST supports equivalence of the tested mean contrast within the prespecified standardized bound of $d=0.30$, while adjusted OLS models show no detectable conditional association after accounting for medium, creation era, and aspect ratio. The paper does not interpret these results as proof of model fairness. Instead, it shows how cohort selection, missing attribution, prompt choice, and coarse score instruments limit what an archival audit can establish.

The manuscript fits the special issue because it connects model auditing with the provenance and representational limits of cultural heritage data. It also gives practical recommendations for reporting cohort construction, testing prompt sensitivity, and calibrating scores within relevant medium strata.
